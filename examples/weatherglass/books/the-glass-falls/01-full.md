*Recorded at the Society's station, 51 feet above mean sea level.*

The barometer in the hall had hung there so long that nobody read it. It was
furniture. Every few days somebody knocked the brass set-hand round with a
knuckle on the way past, more out of habit than instrument-keeping, and that
was the whole of its use.

On the Tuesday the needle stood at 29.6 and the set-hand at 30.1, which is to
say it had fallen half an inch since Sunday. Half an inch in two days is not a
figure a house feels. The sky was ordinary. The washing dried.

It fell another two tenths overnight. By noon on the Wednesday the gulls were
inland — twelve miles inland, walking about on the football pitch like they had
been invited — and the cattle in the low field had all sat down together in the
one corner with their backs to the west. The cat would not go out.

---

Old weather-lore is mostly wrong, and the little of it that is right is right
for a dull reason: animals have better ears than we do, and a falling glass and
a long swell make a sound. *Seagull, seagull, sit on the sand; it's never good
weather while you're on the land* is not prophecy. It is a bird doing arithmetic
about wind, and doing it a day earlier than the household did.

The gale came in at four the next morning from the south-west and took eleven
slates, the greenhouse, and — this is the part the Society records, because
records are what we are for — the barometer itself, which came off its hook when
the front door blew in and landed face down on the tiles without breaking.

It read 28.9. It had been right the whole time.
